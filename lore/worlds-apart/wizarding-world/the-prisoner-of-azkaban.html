The Prisoner of Azkaban is the third story arc of in . It covers ’s third year at , when escapes Azkaban and is believed to be hunting him. Every reputation the year rests on turns out to be false: Black is innocent, the man he was jailed for killing is alive, and the Ministry that imprisoned him without a trial tries to do it again.

Story

Privet Drive

Harry Potter spent the summer before his third year counting the days until he could leave. It ended early and badly. Pushed past his patience by Vernon’s sister Marge, whose cruelty ran to his dead parents, Harry lost his temper and his magic at once, and the woman swelled like a balloon and floated away over the garden. He fled into the night certain he had ruined himself, and was picked up by the Knight Bus, a violently purple triple-decker that rescued stranded witches and wizards. At the Leaky Cauldron the Minister for Magic himself, Cornelius Fudge, waved the whole thing away. A year earlier the Ministry had threatened Harry with expulsion for a spell he hadn’t cast. Now, with a murderer loose and the public frightened, it only wanted him alive and off the streets.

The murderer was Sirius Black, the first prisoner ever to escape Azkaban. He had served the dark wizard who killed Harry’s parents, he was believed to have sold them to their deaths, and the adults around Harry agreed in whispers that he had broken out to finish the job. What none of them mentioned, because the story had hardened into fact long ago, was that Black had never been given a trial. In the chaos at the end of the war, a frightened government had locked him away on the strength of what everyone already believed.

The Dementors

The Hogwarts Express was stopped and searched on the way north by a Dementor, one of the hooded, soul-chilling guards of Azkaban. The Ministry had stationed them around the school to catch Black, over the headmaster’s strong and overruled objection. Where a Dementor passed, warmth drained from the air and a person’s worst memory rose to drown everything else. Harry collapsed, hearing a woman screaming somewhere far away. A tired, shabby passenger, Remus Lupin, drove the creature off, gave Harry chocolate and said nothing about the fainting.

Lupin was the new Defence Against the Dark Arts teacher, and the best Harry ever had. He set the third-years against a Boggart, a shapeshifter that becomes whatever its viewer fears most, and Neville, of all people, won the room: his Boggart was Professor Snape, and Neville sent it off in his grandmother’s hat and handbag to a roar of laughter Snape never forgave. In Divination, Professor Trelawney saw the Grim in Harry’s teacup and predicted his death, and went on predicting it all year.

Buckbeak

The happiest lesson of the autumn was Hagrid’s first as a teacher in his own right, promoted to Care of Magical Creatures. He opened, nervous and proud, with a Hippogriff named Buckbeak, a proud eagle-headed beast that demanded courtesy and returned it, and that took to Harry at once. Then Dracaena Malfoy, careless and showing off, insulted the creature to its face, and it opened her arm. The wound was shallow; her reaction was pure theatre. To anyone watching, she was a spoiled girl milking a scratch to ruin a man she disliked. Harry, who had spent two years learning to read her, saw that she was genuinely frightened of her own blood and had never meant the animal any harm.

The harm came from her father. Lucius Malfoy took his daughter’s injury to the Ministry’s committee for the disposal of dangerous creatures, and a hearing was set that everyone knew how to read. Dracaena spent the weeks that followed visibly wretched. It was the first time Harry saw her ashamed of what her family’s name was doing in her name.

The Fat Lady

On Halloween Sirius Black got inside the castle. He slashed the portrait guarding Gryffindor Tower to ribbons trying to get past her, and the whole school slept in the Great Hall that night while the teachers searched. Everyone took it as proof that Black was hunting Harry. No one yet thought to ask why the only Gryffindor he had tried to reach was the Weasley boy every wizard in Britain would have assumed slept there.

The Dementor Match

In November Gryffindor played Hufflepuff in a howling storm. Hufflepuff’s new captain and Seeker was a fifth-year named Cedric Diggory, as decent as he was good. Halfway through the match a hundred Dementors swept onto the pitch, and Harry fell fifty feet from his broom. He woke in the hospital wing to learn that Cedric had caught the Snitch without seeing what had happened behind him, and had begged for a rematch the moment he found out; that the Whomping Willow had smashed Harry’s broom to splinters; and that Ron, whose house had just won its first match in years, had spent the evening at Harry’s bedside instead of at the party.

Harry asked Lupin to teach him to fight back. His reason wasn’t pride but grief: the scream the Dementors dragged up in him was his mother’s, dying to save him, and he would rather relive it a hundred times in practice than be felled by it when it mattered. Through the winter, in an empty classroom, Lupin taught him the Patronus Charm, a guardian made of a single happy memory.

The Marauder’s Map

Without a signed permission form, Harry was the only third-year barred from the village of Hogsmeade. Fred and George took pity on him and handed over their greatest treasure: the Marauder’s Map, an old parchment that showed every secret passage in the castle and the moving name of every person inside it. It showed its reader what they were looking for, and no one who had ever pored over it had gone looking for a man long believed dead.

Through the map and his father’s Cloak Harry reached Hogsmeade. Invisible there, he saw Dracaena tormenting Ron in the lane, lost his sense of fairness for a moment, and pelted her with mud. She fled humiliated and in tears with no idea who had done it, and Harry’s satisfaction turned to shame before she was out of sight. He went after her alone, dropped the Cloak and apologized, graceless and sincere. It was the first honest moment the two of them had ever shared, and neither of them knew what to do with it.

The Firebolt

At Christmas an unsigned, extravagant broom arrived for Harry: a Firebolt, the fastest in the world. Hermione, sure it came from Black and was cursed, reported it to McGonagall to be stripped and checked. The fight over the broom widened into a bitter cold war between her and Ron over her cat, Crookshanks, who had hunted Ron’s ailing rat, Scabbers, across the castle all year. When Ron found blood on his sheets in the Hufflepuff dormitory and ginger cat hairs on the floor, he stopped speaking to her entirely. Hermione had no one to turn to. She was carrying an impossible timetable, every class the school offered, on the strength of a Time-Turner the Ministry had lent her to sit two lessons at once. It had never once turned for her. She was running herself ragged trying to keep up anyway, and wouldn’t admit it.

Hufflepuff

In February Black got into the castle again, this time into the Hufflepuff dormitories, and Ron woke to find him standing over his bed with a knife. Black fled at Ron’s scream. The school locked itself down tighter, and the question no one could answer grew louder: if Black was hunting Harry, why did he keep going after Ron?

Quidditch

With the Firebolt returned to him, Harry flew against Ravenclaw, whose Seeker was a pretty fourth-year named Chao Zheng, Hermione’s housemate. Harry found it hard to concentrate on the Snitch. Late in the match three hooded shapes rose at the edge of the pitch, and Harry turned and blasted them with his first full Patronus. They were Dracaena, Crabbe and Goyle in black robes, trying to spook him off his broom. It was a nasty, childish stunt, and it earned them a humiliating dressing-down from McGonagall. Harry had begun to tell the difference between her pettiness and real malice, and to notice how rarely, when it counted, she chose the malice.

In the final, Gryffindor beat Slytherin for the Quidditch Cup. Harry and Dracaena raced each other for the Snitch the length of the pitch, and Harry won. Oliver Wood, in his last year, wept openly holding the Cup.

The Lost Appeal

Buckbeak’s appeal was lost before it began. Hagrid’s clumsy, desperate defence had never stood a chance against Lucius Malfoy’s money and name, and a date was set for the execution. In the grounds the next day, cornered by the trio over it, Dracaena was smug on the surface the way her father had taught her to be when she was frightened underneath, and she pushed Hermione one word too far. Hermione, with months of fury and exhaustion behind the blow, struck her across the face. Ron howled with laughter. Harry didn’t laugh.

He had seen her at the mud, and after it, and he had watched her be sick about the creature she pretended not to care about. He had no appetite for watching her get hit. When Ron kept the joke going and saw Harry’s face, both his friends turned on him in astonishment, and the three of them had their first real argument that was about her. Harry couldn’t fully explain it, even to himself. Cruelty aimed at someone already brought low was still cruelty, and it didn’t sit any cleaner on his friends than it did on hers. It wasn’t affection, not yet. It was principle, pointed for once at the person it was least convenient to point it at.

The three split apart for a day or two. It was Ron who closed the distance first, finding Harry alone on the castle steps one evening, and in the talking-out that followed something shifted in him: he saw that what had stopped Harry laughing was not softness but heart, the kind he and Hermione might do well to learn from. The rift closed over. But it had opened, and it would not fully close again.

The Shrieking Shack

On the evening of the execution the trio slipped down to sit with Hagrid, and in the hut Scabbers, alive after all, turned up in a milk jug. On the way back a great black dog seized Ron and dragged him, and his rat, through the roots of the Whomping Willow into the derelict house the villagers called the Shrieking Shack. Harry and Hermione followed, and in that ruined room every reputation the year had rested on turned inside out.

The dog was Sirius Black, an Animagus, and Black was innocent. He had been the Potters’ fiercest friend. The true betrayer was the rat: Peter Pettigrew, a fourth friend of Harry’s father, thought dead a hero for twelve years. He had been the family’s Secret-Keeper and sold them to Voldemort, then faked his own murder, framed Sirius for it, and hidden as a Weasley family’s pet. That was why Sirius had gone for the Weasley boy, and why, finding the Gryffindor portrait the wrong door, he had kept looking until he found the right one. Lupin arrived and completed the picture: a werewolf, a Marauder, the last of the four friends who had made the map as schoolboys. A man judged a monster on a lie, a teacher feared for a condition he couldn’t help, and a traitor safe inside a good name all stood together in that room.

The Lake

It nearly came apart at the last. The full moon rose before Pettigrew could be marched up to the castle; Lupin, who had forgotten his potion that night, transformed; and in the chaos the rat slipped free, taking the only proof of Sirius’s innocence with him. The Dementors closed in at the lake on the broken, defenseless men. Harry, failing, saw a great silver stag drive them back across the water and believed for one impossible moment that his dead father had come. Sirius was taken anyway. Fudge ordered the Dementor’s Kiss without a hearing, a second sentence without a trial for the same man.

The Time-Turner

Dumbledore listened, and sent Harry and Hermione back. That night, for the first time, Hermione’s Time-Turner turned. It had never moved for a timetable, but it moved now, because they meant to undo two injustices caught in the act: a creature condemned for being what it was, and a man condemned for a crime he hadn’t committed. They freed Buckbeak from under the executioner’s axe. They waited by the lake, and when the Dementors came for the earlier Harry, Harry understood at last: there was no father across the water. He cast the stag himself. Then they freed Sirius from the tower window, and he flew to freedom on Buckbeak’s back, still a fugitive, cleared in the eyes of no one who mattered.

Aftermath

Lupin resigned before term was out. Snape let slip at breakfast that he was a werewolf, and parents would not have one teaching their children, however good he had been. Pettigrew was gone, almost certainly to find his old master. Professor Trelawney, in a trance she never remembered, had told Harry that the Dark Lord’s servant would break free that very night and help him rise again. And nobody in the Ministry answered for any of it.

On the train home a tiny owl delivered a letter from Sirius, safe in hiding. The Firebolt had been his, the owl was for Ron, and enclosed was a signed permission form for Hogsmeade. Harry went back to Privet Drive with a godfather somewhere out in the world who loved him, and his first clear sight of how badly the adults in charge of his world could fail the people it was their job to protect.
