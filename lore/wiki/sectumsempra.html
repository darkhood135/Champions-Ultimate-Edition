Sectumsempra is a Dark curse that cuts its target open as if with an invisible blade. It was invented by as a student, who wrote it into the margins of his potions book under the heading “for enemies,” signing himself the Half-Blood Prince. Its wounds bleed heavily enough to kill. The only countercurse that closes them, Vulnera Sanentur, was Snape’s own, and he never taught it to anyone., “The Chase”

It is best known for the spring of 1997, when , who had found it in the Prince’s book that same day without knowing what it did, used it on in a sixth-floor washroom. Snape arrived in time to save her life, but not to spare her the scars., “Sectumsempra”

Effects

Cast at a person, Sectumsempra opens deep gashes across the body, as though an unseen sword had been swung through them. The wounds come all at once and bleed fast. Untreated, a victim can bleed to death within minutes.

The curse can be aimed narrowly. Cast at a wand hand from a distance, it may take a finger or an ear rather than a life, and whatever it severs cannot be put back., “The Seven Potters”

The countercurse

Snape devised a cure alongside the curse: Vulnera Sanentur, a healing countercurse chanted rather than spoken, with the wand drawn slowly along each wound while the words are repeated. It stops the bleeding and closes the cuts. It cannot unmake them: where the wounds were, it leaves patches of scarred skin, and those scars are permanent.

No one else ever knew it. Snape wrote the curse into his book but kept the cure to himself, and when he died in 1998 it died with him. In the wizarding world, people know only that the Prince’s curse could be healed by the Prince. Without the countercurse, dittany and a skilled Healer can slow the bleeding and buy time, but they cannot close the wounds the way Vulnera Sanentur did, and what the curse cuts away stays gone.

Origin

Severus Snape invented Sectumsempra while he was a student at , among a collection of spells he scrawled into the margins of his secondhand copy of Advanced Potion-Making. He signed the book as the Half-Blood Prince, after his mother, Eileen Prince. Beside this spell he wrote only that it was “for enemies.”

The book outlived his schooldays in a cupboard in the Potions classroom. In 1996 Harry Potter, short of a textbook, borrowed it, and over the year the Prince’s notes made him the best brewer in his year and taught him spells nobody else knew., “The Prince”

History

The washroom (1997)

Main article:

In the spring of 1997 Harry followed Dracaena Malfoy on the Marauder’s Map to a sixth-floor washroom, where Moaning Myrtle’s ghost was the only other witness. He found her crying over the task she had been given. When she saw him she fired, and the duel that followed tore the room apart. Then she began a curse he recognized, and bit it off half-spoken. Terrified of what she would cast next, Harry reached for the spell he had read that same day, the one marked “for enemies,” without knowing what it did.

It opened her like a blade. Harry caught her before she hit the floor and held her as she bled, begging her to stay awake, and in that state she apologized to him. Snape burst in, pushed him aside, and chanted the countercurse over her until the wounds closed into scars. Then he made Harry carry her to the hospital wing past a gathering crowd.

Harry admitted to himself that night that he loved her. Soon afterward he burned the Prince’s book himself. He never cast the curse again, and he never fully forgave himself for it., “Personality and traits”

The chase (1997)

Main article:

On the night Dumbledore died, Harry ran after Snape across the burning grounds firing every curse he had, the Prince’s spells among them. Snape blocked each one, and revealed that the spells were his: he was the Half-Blood Prince, and he had written Sectumsempra. That was why he, and only he, had been able to heal it.

The Seven Potters (1997)

Main article:

When the Order of the Phoenix moved Harry from Privet Drive under cover of six decoys, Snape rode among the Death Eaters who ambushed them. He aimed Sectumsempra at a Death Eater’s wand hand and missed, and George Weasley lost an ear. It could not be restored.

Etymology

The incantation joins two Latin words: sectum, “cut”, and semper, “always”. Read together, it means something like “cut forever,” and its scars live up to the name. The countercurse, Vulnera Sanentur, means “may the wounds be healed.”

Changes from Canon

  • Harry uses the curse on Dracaena Malfoy, after she begins the Cruciatus Curse and stops herself. He holds her as she bleeds instead of standing over her.
  • Snape reveals himself as the Half-Blood Prince during the chase, which explains why he alone could heal the curse.
  • The countercurse, Vulnera Sanentur, is known only to Snape and lost when he dies.
  • Its scars are permanent, even when the countercurse is used.

Trivia

  • Harry learned the curse and used it on the same day.
  • The Prince’s whole warning was two words, “for enemies.” Harry used it on the girl he was in love with.
  • Dracaena never blamed Harry for the curse, and she came to be proud of the scars it left. Harry never fully forgave himself; the summer after the war he broke down over them, and she was the one who comforted him.
  • On the washroom floor Snape saw how Harry held her, and it was one of the first things that changed how he saw the boy.
  • In the last battle Voldemort opened Dracaena’s arm from shoulder to wrist with the Elder Wand: the same arm Sectumsempra had laid open in the washroom. It was a different curse, and a different man’s cruelty., “The Last Stand”

Behind the scenes

  • The books never give the countercurse a name; Snape’s chant is heard only as an incantation “almost like song.” The name Vulnera Sanentur comes from the film of Half-Blood Prince, and the Archive adopts it as the countercurse’s name.
  • In canon the curse is used on Draco Malfoy. In this continuity Dracaena takes Draco’s place in that scene, and the washroom becomes the turning point of her story with Harry.
  • The infobox picture is a crop of the art for the Memory , chosen so that the article shows what the curse did through Harry’s face rather than through her wounds.
