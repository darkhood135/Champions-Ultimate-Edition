The Chamber of Secrets

The summer after his first year should have felt like a homecoming, but for Harry Potter it was only a return to the narrow, unwanted life he’d been so relieved to leave behind. Shut up in his aunt and uncle’s house, cut off from the magical world and from friends whose letters never came, he had begun to wonder whether Hogwarts had wanted him back at all — until a trembling, wide-eyed house-elf named Dobby appeared in his bedroom to warn him, in a panic, that he must not return to school, because something terrible was being planned there. When Harry refused, Dobby sabotaged the Dursleys’ dinner party to force the point, and left him locked in his room with bars on the window, further from the world he loved than ever.

He was broken out by friends rather than by magic: Ron Weasley and his brothers arrived in a flying car in the dead of night and spirited him away to the Burrow, the Weasleys’ warm, cluttered, gloriously ordinary home — the first place that had ever felt to Harry like a family. For a few weeks, he was simply happy.

The happiness thinned the moment the new year’s shopping brought everyone to Diagon Alley. At a bookshop signing for the preening celebrity author Gilderoy Lockhart, Harry crossed paths with the Malfoys again. Dracaena needled him and Ron, and began to ease off the moment the rest of the Weasleys closed ranks around their brother — until her father swept in and, with a few cold words, pressed her to sharpen her claws again. It was Harry’s first sight of Lucius Malfoy — elegant, contemptuous, hateful on the instant — and in the ease with which a single word from him brought his daughter back into line, Harry glimpsed the shape of where she had learned it all, reading it, as he would for a long time, as nothing warmer than control. In the scuffle that broke out between Lucius and Ron’s father, no one saw Lucius slip a small black diary into the youngest Weasley’s cauldron.

Barred from the hidden platform on the first of September — Dobby’s work again — Harry and Ron made the reckless choice to chase the train in the flying car, crashing into the vicious tree that guarded the castle grounds and nearly getting themselves expelled before term had even begun. Ron’s wand snapped in the wreck, and would plague him with backfiring spells for the rest of the year.

The term itself soon curdled into something darker. Lockhart proved a vain fraud, and Harry began to hear a cold, murderous voice moving somewhere in the walls that no one else could hear. At Halloween came the first blow: the caretaker’s cat found stiff and petrified beneath a message daubed on the stone — the Chamber of Secrets had been opened, and the enemies of its heir were warned to beware. The whispers turned toward Harry.

Among those happy to fan them was Dracaena, newly installed as Slytherin’s Seeker after her father bought the entire team their brooms. When the Slytherins swaggered onto the pitch to flaunt them and Hermione cut through the display — pointing out that Gryffindor’s players had earned their places rather than purchased them — the moment turned. Dracaena glanced around at her watching housemates, felt the weight of their expectation that she put Hermione in her place, and, after a hesitation that cost her visibly, forced out the ugliest word she knew in a single harsh burst — all to impress them. The word had never really been hers; it was her father’s, her house’s, a thing she recited rather than believed, and the shame of it curdled in her even as it left her mouth. Ron’s furious attempt to curse her rebounded off his broken wand and left him heaving up slugs; Dracaena, her pride stung as much as anything, fled in tears. Harry, who had now looked Lucius Malfoy in the eye, found the whole scene unsettled him more than it satisfied him.

Lessons and long library hours slowly filled in the legend behind the message. Centuries before, Salazar Slytherin — one of the four founders of the school, and the one who believed magic should be withheld from children of non-magical families — was said to have built a hidden chamber within the castle and sealed a monster inside it, to be loosed one day by his true heir, to finish the purge he never could.

The dread sat oddly beside the ordinary rhythms of school — among them the year’s first Quidditch match, Gryffindor against Slytherin, Harry and Dracaena matched Seeker to Seeker. A Bludger, bewitched to hunt Harry alone, battered him the length of the match and shattered his arm. For a moment, seeing the pain he was in, Dracaena faltered — her taunts coming out uncertain, almost soft — before her pride, and the dawning realization that Potter had won the match for Gryffindor anyway, hardened them back into mockery she half-hated herself for. She told herself she was the better player. She did not quite believe that either. Harry caught the Snitch one-handed, then endured Lockhart’s catastrophic attempt to mend the arm, which emptied it of bone entirely. That same night a cheerful first-year, Colin Creevey, was found petrified.

In the fearful weeks that followed, a dueling club was convened, and Harry was set against Dracaena. When she conjured a serpent to menace him, Harry calmed it without thinking — by speaking to it, in the hissing tongue of snakes — and the watching school recoiled, certain now that he was Slytherin’s heir. But Dracaena, rather than crowing, found herself quietly frightened for him: sure he had not done the terrible things himself, and afraid that something dark had sunk its hooks into a boy she was realizing, against all sense, she rather liked exactly as he was. From that day Harry walked the corridors feared and shunned.

Determined to find the real culprit, Hermione brewed a potion that let Harry and Ron borrow the shapes of Dracaena’s hulking companions and slip into the Slytherin common room to question her directly. Pressed to hold forth — a thing she plainly wasn’t used to, being the quietest of the three whenever the mask slipped — Dracaena gave herself away as no one’s mastermind: she didn’t know who the heir was, wished she did, and offered only a resigned, callous hope that if the monster took anyone at all, it might as well be the Granger girl. What Harry saw, behind the bluster, was a lonely child performing for companions who weren’t really friends at all. They came away certain of one thing: whoever had opened the Chamber, it was not Dracaena Malfoy. (Hermione’s own potion went awry, and she spent weeks in the hospital wing covered in cat’s fur.)

The year’s true thread lay elsewhere. Harry came into possession of a blank old diary that wrote back when he wrote in it — the preserved record of a Hogwarts student from fifty years before named Tom Riddle, who drew Harry into a memory of catching the culprit of the Chamber’s last opening: Hagrid, and a monstrous pet spider. Harry’s suspicions turned, painfully, toward his friend.

Then the attacks worsened, and struck close. Hermione herself was found petrified — but not before her relentless research had cracked the mystery: the monster was a basilisk, a giant serpent whose gaze killed outright and petrified when met only in reflection, moving unseen through the castle’s pipes. With the school on the brink of closing, Hagrid was hauled off to the wizard prison on old suspicion, and Dumbledore himself was forced from the castle.

A last clue from Hagrid sent Harry and Ron deep into the Forbidden Forest to follow the spiders, where they found the ancient, enormous Aragog and learned that Hagrid had been innocent all along, that the thing in the Chamber was the one creature spiders feared, and that a girl had died in a bathroom the last time it was loosed. They escaped the spiders’ hunger by the barest margin.

The dead girl, they realized, still haunted that very bathroom — Moaning Myrtle — and the Chamber’s entrance lay hidden within it. But even as they pieced it together, the worst message of all appeared on the wall: Ron’s little sister Ginny had been taken down into the Chamber itself. She had, all year, been the one unknowingly pouring her heart into Riddle’s diary — and it had been feeding on her, blurring her memory and nudging every suspicion away from a quiet first-year, so that no one around her ever thought to look.

Dragging the exposed fraud Lockhart along, the boys found the entrance; Lockhart’s last cowardly bid to flee and wipe their memories backfired off Ron’s broken wand, erasing his own mind instead and collapsing the passage behind them. Harry went on into the Chamber alone.

There he found Ginny cold and fading, and the memory of Tom Riddle grown almost solid on the life he had drained from her — revealed, at the last, as the boy who would become Lord Voldemort, his very name rearranged into the title he would one day make the world afraid to speak. For all that he had hollowed her out, some buried part of Ginny had fought him the whole year through — and it was that resistance, her unknowing hand pulling the serpent’s strikes a fraction wide, that had left every victim frozen rather than slain. Riddle loosed the basilisk. Help came on wings: Dumbledore’s phoenix, bearing the old Sorting Hat, blinded the serpent and yielded up from the Hat a gleaming sword, with which Harry — a venomous fang already buried in his arm — drove the blade through the monster’s skull. With that same fang he struck the diary through, and Riddle, screaming, unravelled into nothing as Ginny woke. The phoenix’s tears drew the venom from Harry’s arm before it could take him.

In the aftermath the pieces came clear. It was Lucius Malfoy who had slipped the cursed diary into Ginny’s things, hoping to reopen the old terror and ruin his enemies in the doing; Harry, with a little cunning, turned the trap back on him and freed the long-suffering Dobby in the bargain. Lucius was stripped of his seat on the school’s governing board, the petrified were revived — Hermione among them — Hagrid came home, and the castle breathed again. For Dracaena, there was only the sour taste of her father brought low in public, by Potter, once more — and, though she would never have admitted it, a whole year’s worth of small moments she could not quite square with the enemy her father insisted he was.
